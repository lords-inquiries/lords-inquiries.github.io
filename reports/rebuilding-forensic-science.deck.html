Rebuilding forensic science for criminal justice: an urgent need

The House of Lords Science and Technology Committee’s report in twelve slides: what it found, six themes and its main recommendations.

“Forensic science is not working for anyone.”

Not for the police, not for forensic scientists, not for lawyers, and ultimately not for the public and the criminal justice system. The forensic scientist Professor Angela Gallop told the committee this; its inquiry “found little to contradict it”.

Six themes

Theme 1 of 6

An independent national forensic service

Police forces now do most forensic work themselves, with patchy oversight and a risk of bias. The Government plans to deliver forensics nationally through a new National Police Service. The committee wants that service kept independent of the police, and set up urgently.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 18Keep the new national forensics service independent of the police, for example with a Scottish-style “sterile corridor”, and start now rather than wait for police mergers.
  • Recommendation 14Require police forces to publish data on their in-house forensics, accreditation and quality incidents, with forensics spending shown as separate budget lines.
  • Recommendation 51Police forces should say whether they will seek accreditation for physical and digital forensics, and the Home Office should publish every force’s status.

£500m+a year: estimated police spending on in-house forensics, over 80% of everything the police spend on forensic services.Paragraph 59

Theme 2 of 6

Equality of arms, and saving at-risk specialisms

Defence experts are a vital check on bad forensic science, but they are underpaid, fragmented and dwindling. Specialisms such as fibres and footprints are dying out. The committee wants fair pay, a register of experts and a real plan to keep rare skills.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 22The Legal Aid Agency should urgently review its rates for forensic experts: at a minimum equal to what the prosecution pays, and paid promptly.
  • Recommendation 23A National Institute for Forensic Science could keep a register of approved experts for the defence, working to the Regulator’s standards.
  • Recommendation 29Make a credible plan to preserve specialisms at crisis point, through retainers, the institute or the national service: “It is time to stop talking about this and do it.”
All paragraphs on this theme

£83an hour: what legal aid pays a defence expert in London (£104 elsewhere). The prosecution pays more than £130.Paragraphs 93 and 99

Theme 3 of 6

A stable, competitive market

In 2019 the committee warned the commercial market was at risk of becoming dysfunctional; it now has. The committee wants a plan to stabilise it, fair prices, room for smaller firms, and a review of what accreditation costs them.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 10Assess the risks of Eurofins’ near-monopoly, stabilise existing provision, lower barriers to new providers and make sure contracts pay a fair price.
  • Recommendation 11Design contracts that bring in small and medium-sized firms, and decide who is responsible for the market’s resilience and oversight.
  • Recommendation 54Review the impact of UKAS accreditation costs, and help small providers achieve accreditation while staying viable.
All paragraphs on this theme

80%+of external forensic science services are provided by one company, Eurofins.Paragraph 55

Theme 4 of 6

A national archive for evidence

Since the Forensic Science Service closed in 2012, evidence has been kept by 43 police forces and several providers, often without the expertise or money to do it well. Missing and damaged evidence is collapsing prosecutions. The committee wants the Forensic Archive reopened for new cases.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 31Take long-term evidence storage away from the police: reopen the Forensic Archive and resource it to take new casework, transfers and more retrievals, possibly with regional hubs.
  • Recommendation 32Meanwhile, police forces should employ forensic archivists, and the National Police Service should set standards and inspect how evidence is stored.
All paragraphs on this theme

30,000prosecutions collapsed between October 2020 and September 2024 due to lost, missing, damaged or insufficient evidence (BBC and University of Leicester analysis).Paragraph 131

Theme 5 of 6

A National Institute for Forensic Science, set up now

No one coordinates research, training or rare expertise across a fragmented system. The committee first called for a national institute in 2019. It says one can be set up now, without waiting for police reform.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 56Establish a National Institute for Forensic Science now, to coordinate research, give impartial advice, keep lists of trusted experts and maintain best-practice guidance.
  • Recommendation 34Make a long-term plan to fund forensic science research; the institute could set research priorities with providers, police, lawyers and academics.
  • Recommendation 45Train the next generation properly: trainees should follow cases from crime scene to courtroom, and the Office for Students should review forensic science degrees.
All paragraphs on this theme

2019is when the committee first recommended a national institute. It was not set up.Paragraph 277

Theme 6 of 6

Digital forensics and AI

Digital evidence keeps growing and backlogs have barely improved since 2019. AI could help analyse huge volumes of data, but deepfakes threaten the reliability of evidence. The committee wants a funded research programme and national tools that courts can trust.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 41Fund a research and development programme on digital evidence: how to store it, how to analyse it in a trusted way, and how to deal with deepfakes.
  • Recommendation 42Say how much of the new £115 million goes to forensics. The national forensics function should provide approved AI tools and procedures that are explainable in court.
All paragraphs on this theme
