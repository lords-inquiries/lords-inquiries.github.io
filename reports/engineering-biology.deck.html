Don’t fail to scale: seizing the opportunity of engineering biology

The House of Lords Science and Technology Committee’s report in 16 slides: what engineering biology is, why the UK’s early lead is slipping, why its companies struggle to grow here, and what the committee wants done.

1st Report of Session 2024–26 (HL Paper 55), published 14 January 2025. An unofficial guide, not a Parliament publication.

“Unless we act quickly, the UK is at a severe risk, once again, of seeing the economic and industrial benefits … exploited overseas.”

The UK led the world in engineering biology after far-sighted investments over a decade ago, but its position has slipped as other countries invest more and plan more strategically (summary).

We were ahead of the game, but we have lost that leadership.”Professor Susan Rosser, Edinburgh Genome Foundry. At the end of the last funding round for six centres of excellence, “the funding dropped off a cliff”, and people they had trained left for jobs overseas (paragraph 25).

Moving atoms as well as bits

Engineering biology is the design and construction of new or modified organisms or molecules, based on those found in nature (summary). Researchers design a biological system, build it with synthetic biology, test it and learn from the results (paragraph 15). AI can rewrite the “software” of our world; engineering biology can provide the “hardware” too (summary).

A platform technology … that sits underneath all sorts of different applications.”Professor Dame Angela McLean, Government Chief Scientific Adviser (paragraph 19).

Reading and writing DNA got cheap

The first human genome sequenced cost around $100 million in 2005; it can now be done for less than $1,000. Making DNA has fallen from $1 a base pair in 1993 to a few cents (paragraph 11). Since 2012, CRISPR has made gene editing simpler and cheaper (paragraph 12).

With machine learning to analyse the data, “new frontiers are now opening” (paragraph 11). Other countries have noticed: “It is a race. It is very competitive” (Professor Paul Freemont, paragraph 36).

Started here, scaled elsewhere

The UK is good at starting companies, but they struggle to grow and often move abroad, especially to the US, to raise money or to list (conclusion 46). “Typically, we have quite good seed money but very poor scale-up money” (Dr Charles Hall, paragraph 173). This failure to scale is a long-standing problem across many technologies; engineering biology is a case study (summary).

Corporate veal: the Americans turn it into beef.”Lord Willetts, on what the UK provides when US investors buy its companies. He wants them rooted deeply enough in the UK that much of the work stays here (paragraph 174).

Seven key areas

The committee groups what the UK must get right into seven areas: strategy, skills, regulation, infrastructure, investment, adoption and governance (summary). Choose one to go to it.

Key area 1 of 7

A plan, £2 billion and a champion

The National Vision (December 2023) promised £2 billion over ten years. Witnesses asked whether it was new money: “If it is rebadged money … we are back to square one” (Professor Susan Rosser, paragraph 39). The Vision also lacks specific outcomes (conclusion 3).

We need to decide what the UK wants to be.”Dr Peter Williams, INEOS (paragraph 27).
  • Recommendation 8Recommit urgently to at least £2 billion over the next decade, and say how it will be split between research, skills and infrastructure, and what it should achieve.
  • Recommendation 6In the industrial strategy, pick the areas where the UK can excel, with specific metrics and outcomes, and report progress regularly.
  • Recommendation 13Appoint a national sector champion: a high-profile figure from industry or academia who can bring government together and lead the strategy.

Key area 2 of 7

Skills and talent

Industry is short of people who can “write DNA, read DNA and understand what DNA does” (paragraph 77), and of technicians: of the more than 260 people one centre has trained in fermentation, “they all get hoovered up” (Mark Bustard, paragraph 87). High visa costs put talent off (conclusion 22).

From Kentucky Fried Chicken to a really highly skilled automation biologist. We want more of that.”Professor Susan Rosser, on an apprentice at the Edinburgh Genome Foundry (paragraph 89).
  • Recommendation 19Through UKRI, urgently fund more doctoral training centres, with most places including a funded year in industry.
  • Recommendation 21Skills England should fund flexible apprenticeships, including degree apprenticeships; UKRI should back a master’s conversion course.
  • Recommendation 23Expand the Global Talent visa beyond a few thousand a year, with more sponsors and room for entrepreneurial, manufacturing and industrial skills.

Key area 3 of 7

A clear route to market

Companies find it too hard to know which regulators will oversee them and what the route to market is (summary). Industry did not know who was in the regulators’ network set up in 2023; asked, the Government Chief Scientific Adviser said “even I do not know” (paragraph 112).

The recommended timelines are about half what they are in the UK.”Will Milligan of Extracellular, on approvals in Singapore (paragraph 106).
  • Recommendation 26Publish a map of which regulator covers which products and a streamlined route to approval, with a single point of contact; make the network’s members public (recommendation 27).
  • Recommendation 29Resource regulators to assess new technologies early and swiftly, with timelines that compete with other countries.
  • Recommendation 33Work with the National Physical Laboratory, the British Standards Institution, industry and ISO to develop standards, and encourage data-sharing.

Key area 4 of 7

From lab bench to fermenter

Useful labs are under-used because start-ups do not know about them or cannot afford them (summary). Biofoundries were funded for equipment but not for staff, so they must charge more (paragraph 144). Companies that outgrow UK pilot plants go to European ones, “and they tend not to come back for manufacturing” (Mark Bustard, paragraph 164).

Batteries not included.”Professor Nick Talbot, on a model that funds equipment but not the people to run it (paragraph 146).
  • Recommendation 38Open national and university labs to start-ups that need data for patents, with Innovate UK funding to help them do it.
  • Recommendation 41Fund greater use of the biofoundries and the Centre for Process Innovation, through block grants or grants for users, as the EU does for Bio Base Europe.
  • Recommendation 43DSIT should map the facilities that exist and their terms of use, and remove barriers to using them.

Key area 5 of 7

Capital to scale

There are “not many investors in the UK with those deep pockets” for scale-up (Dr Martin Turner, paragraph 191), and it is unclear whether the National Wealth Fund can invest in engineering biology (conclusion 48). Pensions investing less at home risk a ‘doom loop’ (conclusion 52).

If you are going to raise money for a tech company in the UK you take your CFO and if you are going to raise money in the US you take your CTO.”Lord Willetts: US investors want the technology story (paragraph 192).
  • Recommendation 49Widen the scope and scale of the National Wealth Fund to cover engineering biology, with a specialist investment team.
  • Recommendation 51Join up funding across the ‘valley of death’, from research councils and Innovate UK to the National Wealth Fund and British Patient Capital; consider capital grants outside medicine.
  • Recommendation 53Make the Mansion House pension reforms more ambitious and faster, and help consolidated funds back small UK tech companies.

Key area 6 of 7

Buy it, and make a market

Public procurement is a third of public spending, £292 billion a year (paragraph 58). The US BioPreferred programme buys bio-based products, giving companies the confidence to invest at scale (paragraph 59). Without incentives, large companies will not leave cheaper fossil-based processes (summary).

A purchase is worth 10 times a grant.”Professor Dame Angela McLean, recalling the Ministry of Defence’s purchase of a UK-made quantum computer (paragraph 65).
  • Recommendation 16Use public procurement, learning from BioPreferred; consider reserving a mandatory share of procurement budgets for innovative UK SMEs and new technologies.
  • Recommendation 57Give manufacturers, such as chemical and fossil fuel companies, reasons to switch: taxes on carbon, pollutants or waste, and mandates where a bio-based alternative could scale.
  • Recommendation 58Start by mandating that a small share of production uses bio-based processes, and raise it over time.

Key area 7 of 7

Trust and biosecurity

Engineering biology is dual-use: bad actors could use it to make or enhance pathogens, more so as it gets cheaper (conclusion 64). Public awareness is limited, and past controversies over genetically modified organisms show what is at stake (conclusion 62).

If we do not know what normal is, we do not know what unusual is.”Piers Millett, International Biosecurity and Biosafety Initiative for Science, on why monitoring matters (paragraph 255).
  • Recommendation 63Support public engagement on products coming to market; regulators should explain what they regulate and why.
  • Recommendation 65Commit to the Biological Security Strategy’s biosurveillance and population testing, and report on its progress by June 2025.
  • Recommendation 71With international partners, make screening of DNA synthesis orders mandatory in the UK, backed by export controls and treaties.

“We have many of the ingredients to make this a success. But this reaction requires a catalyst.”

Engineering biology could help the UK with health, sustainability and climate change, and grow the economy. But Lord Vallance indicated that the window of opportunity is small and closing. “We cannot afford to miss it” (summary).
