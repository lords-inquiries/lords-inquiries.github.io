Bleeding to death: the science and technology growth emergency

The House of Lords Science and Technology Committee’s report in 17 slides: the evidence, what the committee concluded, and what it wants done.

2nd Report of Session 2024–26 (HL Paper 192), published 5 November 2025.

Go straight to all 70 conclusions and recommendations

Scroll, swipe or use the arrow keys. Each drop along the cut is one of the report’s 70 numbered conclusions and recommendations, longer for longer paragraphs. Solid drops are recommendations, hollow ones conclusions. Point at one to read it.

“The UK economy is bleeding out in a national crisis of growth.”

The committee says the UK’s failure to scale its science and technology companies has “reached a crisis point”. A procession of promising companies has moved overseas rather than grow here, and the UK’s inability to keep the economic benefits of its research is “a fatal flaw in any growth strategy”.

From the report’s summary. The inquiry heard from 40 witnesses and received over a hundred pieces of written evidence (paragraph 10).

Good at starting companies, poor at scaling them

In 2014 the OECD ranked the UK third in the world for start-ups and 13th for scaling up. A decade on, “many of the same fundamental challenges remain” (paragraph 19).

Early-stage money is in relatively good health. The shortage comes when a company needs hundreds of millions to grow, and it looks abroad, usually to the US (paragraph 57).

A doom loop

“The UK is in a ‘doom loop’: its capital pools are shallow, its institutional investors risk-averse, and its technology companies undervalued.” Start-ups multiply, struggle to raise scale-up money at home, and move overseas to find it. The committee warns of an ‘incubator economy’ (conclusion 2, paragraph 28).

Seven themes

Six follow the headline recommendations in the committee’s summary; the seventh, keeping companies in the UK, comes from its second chapter. Choose one to go to it. Each strip is the report’s 70 drops, with the theme’s paragraphs in red.

Theme 1 of 7

Keep companies in the UK

It is nobody’s responsibility.”Ceri Smith, Director General of the Office for Investment, when asked whose job it is to try to keep technology companies in the UK (paragraph 52).

The committee calls the Government’s approach to overseas takeovers “notably laissez-faire”, and says rising conflict and fast-moving technology now make critical companies a matter of national interest, if not national security (conclusions 10 and 11).

The committee recommends

  • Recommendation 12Tell new investment apart from takeovers; run exit surveys and founder interviews when companies list or sell abroad; decide which technologies the UK must control, and take equity stakes through public investment bodies.

Theme 2 of 7

Put UK capital to work

UK workplace pension schemes manage over £2 trillion (paragraph 59), yet their investment in UK companies has collapsed. The committee calls the fall “shocking”, and says that because its origins are largely political, it can be reversed politically.

Anybody who thinks this is going to be fixed by Mansion House needs their head examined.”Professor Sir John Bell (paragraph 75).
  • Recommendation 16Insist that pension schemes consolidate; track how much reaches UK science and technology; set targets for the end of 2027 and keep the power to mandate; ask members how much they want invested in the UK; consider clawing back tax relief.
  • Recommendation 17The Pensions and Science ministers and the British Business Bank should make sure the reforms reach innovative companies, not only other assets.

Theme 2 of 7, continued

A drought in London’s stock market

Companies list in New York expecting higher valuations, a perception that “may not be entirely borne out” (paragraph 20). Each one that goes “reduces activity and capital in the UK market, perpetuating a vicious cycle” (report summary).

  • Recommendation 20Steer capital into UK markets: shift ISA limits from cash towards shares, a stamp duty holiday for listings, new savings vehicles for UK science and technology, and campaigns for financial literacy.
  • Recommendation 22Keep R&D tax credits at current levels and tackle the fraud rather than recast the scheme; reuse money recovered for scale-up support.

R&D tax credits cost about £7.5 billion in 2022/23, when error and fraud were estimated at 17.6% (paragraphs 107 and 109).

Theme 3 of 7

Buy from innovators

The most valuable thing for any company we can invest in is not us investing more in that company; it is the company getting a purchase order.”Saul Klein, venture investor (paragraph 117).

Public bodies spent £434 billion on procurement in 2024/25, but risk aversion and a habit of favouring large incumbents shut innovative small firms out (conclusion 23). As Lord O’Donnell put it: “No one ever got fired for buying IBM” (paragraph 120).

  • Recommendation 25A mandatory share of every department’s and local authority’s procurement budget for innovative UK small firms, rising to 3%; more Contracts for Innovation; outcome-based, stage-gated contracts; ministers who back officials when innovation fails; a presumption in favour of buying British.

Theme 4 of 7

One public investment pipeline, at scale

Innovate UK, the British Business Bank and the National Wealth Fund each back companies at a different stage, but the landscape is “fragmented, overly complicated, and small in scale”, and companies fall through a ‘leaky pipeline’ (conclusion 26). One investor described “pinballing around” between them (paragraph 135).

  • Recommendation 27At the very least, work much more closely, make clear who does what, and track referrals from one body to the next as a key performance indicator.
  • Recommendation 29Put the British Business Bank on a statutory footing, scale it and the National Wealth Fund up significantly, and consolidate them, with a strong case for including Innovate UK.
  • Recommendation 39Give the National Wealth Fund a target for the number of late-stage funding rounds it backs in each Parliament.

Theme 5 of 7: talent, networks and regulation

The red carpet, not red tape

Up-front visa costs for scientists are around 17 times those in other research-intensive countries: over £20,000 for a family of four on a five-year Global Talent visa (paragraphs 185 and 189). The committee calls the Home Office’s unwillingness to review the barriers “an absurd act of national self-harm” (conclusion 43).

  • Recommendation 44Use the Global Talent Taskforce and a much bigger Global Talent Fund, and do not let the Home Office block the abolition of fees for top talent.
  • Recommendation 45Let the Immigration Health Surcharge be paid over time, make attracting talent a primary Home Office goal, and review the Scale-up Worker visa.
  • Recommendation 54A concierge service with the power to clear blockages for high-potential companies, and a regulatory front door for innovators (recommendation 59).

Theme 6 of 7

Protect the research base

University research is not funded at full cost and leans on fees from overseas students, whose numbers are falling, while inflation has eroded the real value of tuition fees by a third (paragraph 250). A planned levy on international students will make things worse, the committee says (conclusion 60).

‘We want 30% of the company, royalties and licensing fees.’ That company is dead on arrival.”Dr Patrick Rose of Germany’s innovation agency, SPRIND, on some universities’ terms for spin-outs (paragraph 257).
  • Recommendation 61Set out a long-term plan to restore university finances, particularly for research. An international student levy is “likely to be self-defeating”.
  • Recommendation 63Research funders such as UKRI should track the patents and spin-outs they support; keep implementing the independent review of spin-outs.

Theme 7 of 7

Leadership from the top

None of this will succeed, the committee says, without leadership from the Prime Minister and Chancellor. Its central recommendation is a National Council for Science, Technology and Growth, modelled on the National Security Council: meeting often, settling conflicts between departments and deciding which technologies the UK must control.

We have a constitution that is all about departments, and there is very little about cross-department work.”Lord O’Donnell, former Cabinet Secretary (paragraph 25).
  • Recommendation 68Set up the council with the Prime Minister, Chancellor, Science Minister and the main departments, as a real forum for decisions, not a rubber stamp.
  • Recommendation 69Give it the prominence, resources and working practices of the National Security Council.
  • Recommendation 9The Treasury must take responsibility for driving growth, and use its Green Book review to value the long-term returns from R&D.

Vital signs, published every six months

To keep up the pressure, the committee wants the Government to track these measures, publish them every six months, and use any that lag to spur action through the council and cross-government committees (recommendations 66 and 67).

My desire would be to make as many of those things public as possible so that we can hold our own feet to the fire.”Lord Vallance, then Minister for Science (paragraph 265).

Who is asked to act

Point at a body to light up its recommendations along the strip. Choose one to read them in the explorer.

“The most likely candidate to become the Government’s ‘trillion-dollar tech company by 2035’ probably already exists.”

The Government must stop the bleeding, the committee says: convince that company, and dozens of others looking overseas, that the best future for them is in the UK. If it helps entrepreneurs, unlocks investment, changes the culture around innovation and organises its efforts, “it can still reap enormous rewards for the nation” (conclusion 70).
