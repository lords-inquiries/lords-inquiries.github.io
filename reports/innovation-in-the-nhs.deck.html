Innovation in the NHS: Personalised Medicine and AI—lost opportunities?

The House of Lords Science and Technology Committee’s report in twelve slides: what it found, its six priorities and its main recommendations.

“The NHS does not have an innovation problem, but it struggles to adopt proven innovations effectively.”

That is the committee’s central finding. The NHS has many clinicians who want to innovate, and no shortage of new ideas. What fails is adoption: unclear lines of accountability, poor structures to promote and budget for innovation, and procurement repeated trust by trust.

200+NHS trusts, each running its own procurement and relying on different evidence to adopt something new.Report summary

Six priorities

The committee sums up its recommendations under six headings that cut across the report’s chapters. Choose one to go to it. Each strip shows where that priority’s paragraphs sit in the report.

Priority 1 of 6

Fund and commission innovations that work at a national scale

Too many innovations get stuck as pilots in a single trust, with no route to routine use. The committee wants proven technologies commissioned and bought once, nationally or regionally, and spread to every trust.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 23Replace “haphazard and proliferating pilots” with a proof-of-value process that leads to sustained adoption, shift funding towards implementation, and ringfence trusts’ innovation budgets.
  • Recommendation 25Make the Genomic Medicine Service the template for other priority innovations: national priorities and commissioning, regional delivery, and a clear route from evidence to routine use.
  • Recommendation 27Buy priority technologies through national or regional frameworks, with standard contracts and a funding route from one trust to many, and expand “passporting” between trusts.

Priority 2 of 6

Build a sovereign health data capability for the UK

The UK’s health data are exceptionally rich, but access for research has gone backwards since the pandemic. The committee wants the new Health Data Research Service properly backed as a single front door, with UK-owned capacity to store and analyse the data.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 41Back the Health Data Research Service with sustained, long-term investment. It should publish a strategy and success metrics and be a single front door to NHS data, UK Biobank and Our Future Health.
  • Recommendation 44Set out a framework for commercial partnerships, “fair trade rather than free trade in health data”, and justify the self-funding model so UK SMEs and researchers keep access.
  • Recommendation 53Set a clear direction on how UK health data can be used to train clinical AI models, and develop a sovereign AI capability for health.

Priority 3 of 6

Roll out personalised medicine with supporting evidence

Genomics is strongest at diagnosing and treating disease, especially cancer, and weaker at predicting it in healthy people. The committee says: take the near-term wins now, and build the evidence before relying on prediction.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 7NHS England should publish a pharmacogenomics implementation plan, starting with high-impact drug–gene interactions and the prevention of severe adverse drug reactions.
  • Recommendation 2Build the evidence on newborn genomic screening and polygenic risk scores before rolling them out, with enough genomic counsellors and training.
  • Recommendation 5Work out how many pathologists, oncologists and radiologists are needed for cancer genomics results to arrive in time to guide first treatment.

Priority 4 of 6

Invest in the workforce and clinical trials

Time is the greatest constraint on clinicians’ research, training and innovation, and the clinical academics who bridge research and care are in decline. The committee wants protected time, a workforce plan fit for genomics and AI, and faster clinical trials.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 69Give clinicians, whether consultants or not, contractually protected time for research, training and implementing innovation.
  • Recommendation 75Don’t assume AI will cut headcount in the short to medium term. Plan for pharmacogenomics-trained pharmacists, genomic counsellors and data scientists, and modernise training.
  • Recommendation 67Speed up recruitment to clinical trials, reduce the regulatory burden once they run, and let funders experiment with trial design, including AI.
All paragraphs on this priority

6%fewer clinical academics than in 2012, while the number of NHS consultants grew by 50%.Paragraph 300

Priority 5 of 6

Provide clear accountability and better budgeting for innovation

It is often unclear who is responsible for adopting innovation, and annual budgets struggle with one-off costs for treatments that pay back over many years. The committee wants named accountability and budgets that look further ahead.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 31Give each trust a single accountable contact for innovation, and the DHSC a named Senior Responsible Owner for adoption nationally, with adoption tracked trust by trust.
  • Recommendation 33Use outcome-based payment and multi-year budgets for curative and advanced therapies where they are cost-effective in the long run.
  • Recommendation 58The DHSC should tell NICE how to weigh wider social and economic benefits, such as prevention and productivity, in its appraisals.
All paragraphs on this priority

£1.65mper treatment for a potentially curative gene-editing therapy for sickle-cell anaemia.Paragraph 143

Priority 6 of 6

Modernise and clarify regulation for AI in healthcare

AI tools are being developed faster than the rules can keep up, and clinicians are unsure which tools are safe and where liability lies. The committee wants the MHRA able to approve and monitor AI throughout its life, and clear guidance for clinicians.

Main recommendations

  • Recommendation 61Give the MHRA the powers it needs, through legislation if necessary, to approve software and AI as medical devices and to keep monitoring them once they are in use.
  • Recommendation 63The DHSC, royal colleges and regulators should give clinicians practical guidance on using AI: approved tools, human oversight, liability and audit.
  • Recommendation 65Decide whether the Government or the MHRA regulates general-purpose chatbots used for medical purposes, and research how well they perform.
