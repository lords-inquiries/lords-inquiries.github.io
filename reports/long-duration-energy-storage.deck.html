Long-duration energy storage: get on with it

The House of Lords Science and Technology Committee’s report in 17 slides: why the UK must store energy for days, weeks and months, and what the committee wants done.

1st Report of Session 2023–24 (HL Paper 68), published 13 March 2024.

“The long-duration storage replaces fossil fuels as back-up stores of energy.”

Deploying it at scale is “critical to ensure the UK can maintain energy security and reach Net Zero”, the committee says, but it is not being treated with sufficient urgency. Today the back-up is gas, and the UK keeps far less of it than its neighbours. The energy crisis that began in 2021 was “an object lesson” in the UK’s vulnerability (conclusion 2).

From the report’s summary.

More electricity, more weather

To reach Net Zero, heating and transport move onto electricity, and most of it will come from wind and sun. The Climate Change Committee expects demand to rise by half by 2035 and to double by 2050 (box 3). Supply and demand will both be larger and more variable (summary), and both depend more and more on the weather (box 3).

Three different demand profiles: daily, weekly and seasonal.”Caroline Still of Aurora Energy Research, on where storage will be needed; variations in demand will “become more extreme” (paragraph 15).

Dark and still: the dunkelflaute

Germans call it the dunkelflaute. The report calls it the “dark doldrums”: an extended period of low wind in winter, when solar output is low too (paragraph 133). The Royal Society modelled 37 years of weather and found “periods with virtually no wind” and “three consecutive years where demand would significantly outstrip the average supply” (paragraph 49).

Its estimate of the storage needed, 100 TWh, is higher than others partly because it looked across so many years rather than one ‘typical’ year, and partly because it assumed a grid of only wind, solar and storage (box 3).

The caverns along the foot of the screen follow the store.

Power on demand, not just energy in store

A store has to hold enough energy (TWh) and release it fast enough (GW) when it is needed. The Government’s targets should say both (recommendation 8). In the Climate Change Committee’s 2035 scenario, industry, shipping and other steady uses take around 10 GW of hydrogen every day, while ordinary power stations burning it swing from almost nothing to around 24 GW (Figure 4).

Point at a day to read it.

Our neighbours share our weather

Links to other countries’ grids are “very important”, but “European weather cycles are broadly similar to ours”: when wind is low here, it is likely to be low across the continent (paragraphs 20 and 137). The committee warns against over-reliance on them “in an energy crisis which is likely to be Europe-wide” (conclusion 50).

If we are all suffering from a shortage of electricity, I very much doubt that they will give it to us.”Professor Sir Peter Bruce, Royal Society (paragraph 20).

Seven themes

Six follow the key recommendations in the committee’s summary, with its call to engage the public folded into hydrogen; the seventh, the grid, draws on its fourth chapter. Choose one to go to it.

Theme 1 of 7

Ride out the dunkelflaute

Commercial stores make money by charging and discharging often, so there is no guarantee they will be full when a long, still spell arrives (conclusion 16). The National Infrastructure Commission recommends a strategic reserve of 25 TWh by 2040 (paragraph 51). The Government has made no clear commitment.

We do not provide reserves on most of the things in the British economy … Nearly always, the answer is no.”Graham Stuart MP, the minister who gave evidence. He added: “if we need [a strategic reserve], we will bring one forward” (paragraph 55).
  • Recommendation 17Commit to a strategic reserve of stored energy on the scale the National Infrastructure Commission suggests, owned by the Government or contracted out, with terms and funding worked out with industry.
  • Recommendation 18If unabated gas is to play a role, explain how that fits climate targets. Without a reserve, explain how the system will cope with shortfalls like those the Royal Society found.

Theme 2 of 7

Set a target in terawatt-hours

Witnesses agreed that tens of terawatt-hours of long-duration storage will be needed, “several orders of magnitude” more low-carbon storage than the UK has (conclusion 5). The Government has set no target. Officials told the committee: “at the moment, we do not have a … ‘no-regrets’ level” (paragraph 21).

Whether it is 20 or 100 [TWh] … does not really matter.”Daniel Murrant of the Energy Systems Catapult; at the moment the UK has “fractions of a terawatt hour” (paragraph 16).
  • Recommendation 6Set an explicit minimum target for “no-regrets” long-duration storage by 2035, with interim milestones that work back from 2035.
  • Recommendation 8State storage targets in both power (GW) and energy (TWh), including the 10 GW hydrogen production target.
  • Recommendation 10Define medium- and long-duration storage, publish an Assessment of Likely Need by the end of 2024, and support technologies beyond batteries.

Theme 3 of 7

Grid capacity and flexibility

When the lines south are full, wind farms in the north are turned down and gas plants in the south turned up, at a cost of about £1.6 billion in 2022 by one estimate (paragraph 9). Storage can soak up the surplus and ease congestion (conclusion 1), but projects queue for years to connect. One was offered a connection date of November 2037 (paragraph 111).

  • Recommendation 42Ofgem and the system operator should cut the connection queue urgently, report progress, and consider moving storage projects up the queue.
  • Recommendation 57Take the strategic decisions on electricity and hydrogen networks now, set them out in the Strategic Spatial Energy Plan, and deliver them through a cross-departmental Ministerial Working Group.
  • Recommendation 53Ofgem and the system operator should find ways to reward flexible heating; build heat storage into Energy Performance Certificates, VAT relief and grant schemes.

Theme 4 of 7

Make storage investable

Storage pays its way by buying cheap and selling dear, which rewards frequent cycling, not energy held for weeks. Michael Liebreich told the committee there was “almost no investment going into” storage longer than 48 hours (paragraph 37). The Government is consulting on a cap and floor; the committee wants the details finalised “as a matter of urgency” (recommendation 14).

  • Recommendation 14Finalise the business models; design the cap and floor for storage across hours and across days and weeks, and open it to hydrogen.
  • Recommendation 15Fund the scheme enough to deploy at scale, and set minimum targets for the capacity it should support.
  • Recommendation 40Say which services the grid needs, from capacity to duration, and pay storage for providing them on top of the revenue floor.
  • Recommendation 37Give flow and iron-air batteries a clearer role in battery policy, and fund grid-scale chemistries, not just lithium-ion.

Theme 5 of 7

Hydrogen, the long-duration store

For storage over weeks and months, the front-runner is hydrogen: made from water with surplus wind and solar, stored underground and burned for power when needed. The parts are proven, but no UK facility does all three at scale (conclusion 19).

I would want to build a demonstrator … to learn what we need to do when we come to scale it.”Professor Sir Peter Bruce, Royal Society (paragraph 61).
  • Recommendation 20Commission a pilot that combines electrolysis, salt-cavern storage on the scale of hundreds of GWh and hydrogen power, near the existing clusters, and publish what it learns.
  • Recommendation 24Say what hydrogen is for, and decide sooner than 2026 on hydrogen for home heating; the committee advises against it.
  • Recommendation 29Research public acceptability within six months and run a public campaign on hydrogen’s role and safety; it “cannot be left to developers”.

Theme 6 of 7

Pace, not perfection

Storage typically takes 7–10 years to deliver (conclusion 11), so the decisions for 2035 are needed now. “If it waits until it knows precisely how much storage is needed, it will be too late to build it” (summary). A lack of green skills, grid queues and planning delays all put the timeline at risk (summary).

The worst decision that we can make is to … not make those decisions.”Rachel Hay, Climate Change Committee (paragraph 34).
  • Recommendation 12Pace, not perfection: bring forward support schemes and no-regrets investments as soon as possible.
  • Recommendation 44Reform and resource the planning system, recognise storage for its own value by the end of 2024, and set out how onshore hydrogen storage will be regulated.
  • Recommendation 46Work out how many skilled people the hydrogen targets need, and fund training now: it has the longest lead time.

Theme 7 of 7

A plan and a guiding mind

Many bodies have a hand in storage, and “historically, the energy system has been quite siloed” (paragraph 144). The committee welcomes the Strategic Spatial Energy Plan, but warns it could become “a dead letter” if the Government cannot coordinate everyone to deliver it. The system needs “a guiding mind capable of making key strategic decisions” (summary).

A plan that all … stakeholders can focus on … will provide a focal point.”Nick Winser, Electricity Networks Commissioner (paragraph 147).
  • Recommendation 55Make the bodies coordinate, with clear remits; clarify who answers for security of supply; empower and resource the system operator.
  • Recommendation 59The plan should include sites for long-duration storage and its infrastructure, hydrogen and a strategic reserve, and be updated regularly.
  • Recommendation 60The Government must take the plan’s decisions swiftly and commit every actor to them; Ofgem should endorse it.

Who is asked to act

Point at a body to light up its recommendations along the strip. Choose one to read them in the explorer.

“Time is running out for the UK to secure that brighter future.”

The prize, the committee says, is clear: cheaper Net Zero electricity, a strong domestic energy storage industry, and an economy insulated from volatile energy markets and imported fossil fuels. “We urge the Government to take action now” (summary).

What has happened since

  1. 16 May 2024The Government responds: no minimum target yet, a strategic reserve to be considered, and the cap and floor designed by the end of 2024. Read the response
  2. 1 October 2024The National Energy System Operator, the Future System Operator the report discusses, takes over from the ESO in public ownership. NESO
  3. October 2024The Government confirms a cap and floor scheme for long-duration electricity storage, run by Ofgem. Ministers commission the first Strategic Spatial Energy Plan from NESO.
  4. 13 December 2024The Clean Power 2030 Action Plan aims for 4–6 GW of long-duration storage by 2030, alongside 23–27 GW of batteries. GOV.UK
  5. 9 January 2025The House of Lords debates the report.
  6. April to September 2025Ofgem opens the first window; 171 projects apply and 77 pass its eligibility checks.
  7. 26 June 2026Ofgem is minded to support 16 projects, about 7.6 GW, using pumped hydro, compressed air, lithium-ion and flow batteries. Ofgem
  8. October 2026The deadline for the first Strategic Spatial Energy Plan moves from the end of 2026 to the end of 2027. GOV.UK
